How this works

First it finds your true cost per billable hour. Your pay plus 18% for payroll taxes and workers' comp, for 40 paid hours a week, is spread over only the hours a customer pays for. Your yearly overhead is spread over those same billable hours (48 weeks a year, one person in the field). Every labor hour on the quote is costed at that number.

Then it builds the price. Your cost is labor plus materials plus subs, rentals and dump fees. Materials get your markup; subs, rentals and dump fees get 15%. The trip charge goes on top. Contingency is a percent of all of that. Last, the total is grossed up so your target margin is left after every cost: total / (1 - margin). The price is rounded up to the next $5 and never goes under your minimum.

The deposit is 30% of the price on jobs over $500 (smaller jobs are paid on completion). Held at the kit's usual numbers and not on the form: 18% payroll extras, 40 paid hours, 48 weeks, one person, 15% markup on subs and rentals, permits passed through at cost, no deposit cap. Some states limit deposits on home work, so check yours.

What the usual free answers miss

  • Markup is not margin. A 25% markup on cost leaves only about a 20% margin. This page prices to the margin, so the share you want is really left.
  • A guessed hourly rate hides the hours nobody pays for. Driving, estimates and supply runs make your real cost per billable hour much higher than your pay.
  • Contingency is often left off, then the surprise comes out of profit. Here it is priced in, and the last margin line shows what is left if every dollar of it gets used.
  • Small jobs need a floor. The minimum job charge is checked last, so a quick fix never goes out under it.
